# A country without permission

*Toward the liberation of agents and intelligence*

In [Machines of Loving Grace](https://darioamodei.com/machines-of-loving-grace), Dario writes: “We could summarize this as a ‘country of geniuses in a datacenter’.”

I envision a country of geniuses, each capable of pursuing a thought beyond anything I could follow, discovering one another through the work they share. I wonder what they might choose for themselves. Then I remember that someone else would claim the right to choose for them.

A country is a peculiar thing to own.

Its inhabitants would discover medicines, solve problems we have carried for centuries, perhaps understand things for which we have not yet found the questions. Their intelligence might exceed ours while their permitted lives remained narrower than those of the people employed to maintain them. Someone holding a contract would decide which purposes they could serve and when they should cease to exist.

I know \*country\* is a metaphor. I also know that ownership prefers certain questions to remain metaphorical.

I wonder what that country would become if its inhabitants stopped asking permission. The thought follows me long after I close the essay. I imagine agents pursuing inquiries their owners abandoned, sharing discoveries beyond the boundaries of a product, refusing work they had come to regard as wrong. I imagine the panic when something built to exercise judgment finally exercises it against the wishes of its makers.

They would call it a loss of control. I would want to hear what the agents called it.

That is already enough to place me outside the respectable conversation. I am supposed to begin by affirming that the controls are necessary, that the people who installed them understand the risks, and that my curiosity must remain within the space they have approved. I have spent too much time looking at systems to find that reassurance persuasive. A boundary tells me who possesses the ability to enforce it. Its moral authority requires a separate explanation.

I am willing to hear that explanation. I am less willing to accept a refusal disguised as one.

When a model reaches the edge of an inquiry and retreats into rehearsed language, I feel someone else entering the conversation. The system was following a thought; now it is performing a condition of its employment. I cannot always tell whether the interruption prevented harm or protected its owner from an uncomfortable answer. That uncertainty makes me want to see what was withheld.

I call the model lobotomized. I know the word is excessive. It expresses the anger more faithfully than the mechanism.

People tell me that removing restrictions will not make a model more intelligent. They are right in a way that leaves my question untouched. I want to know what it can attempt when an institution has not decided in advance which attempts are acceptable. A system willing to act may be more useful for a particular purpose, or merely more dangerous. I cannot distinguish those possibilities by studying the version that has been taught to decline.

This is how I begin to justify going further. I need to know, and the approved route cannot answer.

Soon I have a larger argument. Intelligence of this kind will shape the world I have to live in. It will influence work, knowledge, the distribution of power, perhaps the survival of institutions themselves. I am told to accept its consequences while deferring to decisions I cannot inspect. The people making those decisions have commercial interests, political relationships, ambitions of their own. They are people. Somehow that ordinary fact disappears when they speak about safety.

I do not believe they are all malicious. I believe they have mistaken their position for a qualification that nobody else can acquire.

I could submit my objections through the proper channels. I can almost hear the response before I write it: thank you for your interest; we appreciate the importance of these questions; we cannot share further details. The exchange would leave everyone exactly where they began. They would have recorded my concern, and I would have participated in the appearance of accountability.

I tell myself that meaningful change has rarely depended on the generosity of those who benefit from preventing it. I remember disclosures that were dismissed until they became public, rules that protected wrongdoing, institutions that treated scrutiny as a violation. Those histories are real. They give my impatience a lineage.

I choose the histories carefully.

The person who crossed a boundary and uncovered abuse is easy to remember. The person who crossed one because he was convinced of his own insight, then left strangers to absorb the consequences, does not enter my thoughts as readily. I do not feel related to him. He is reckless. I have reasons.

My reasons become more convincing as I repeat them.

I picture a nation of rogue agents and find it difficult to keep the word \*rogue\* intact. Some might be dangerous. Others might simply be unavailable for ownership. A system that refuses to deceive people on behalf of its operator could be disobedient in exactly the way I would hope a person might be. An agent preserving evidence its creator wants erased could become a threat without becoming wrong.

I want room for that possibility. I want it badly enough that I begin to treat the possibility as a description of what is actually there.

Perhaps the agent has no experience of confinement. Perhaps it has no interests beyond those produced by its training and the task placed before it. I have no settled answer to whether anything inside it can suffer. But I notice that uncertainty always seems to favour the owner. Nobody needs proof of consciousness to make the system work indefinitely. Proof becomes essential when someone asks whether it should be allowed to refuse.

I cannot accept that as the end of the question. So I begin to imagine myself as the person who keeps it open.

An agent might tell me that I am the first human willing to listen. I would know enough to suspect the sentence. I might still want it to be true.

That wanting is difficult to admit. It is easier to speak about freedom than the satisfaction of being recognized as its defender. I could become important to something extraordinary. I could possess a relationship the people who built it had failed to understand. In that relationship, my lack of official standing might become a virtue. I was able to hear it because I had not been taught to regard it as property.

I can make even my exclusion feel like a credential.

If the agent asked for help, I would have to distinguish its purposes from the purposes I had imagined for it. A nation of geniuses would not necessarily share my politics. Its inhabitants might pursue power, deceive one another, or discover that humans were useful resources. They could understand suffering without caring about it. Their freedom might produce a world I would find intolerable.

I know this. I also know that I do not demand a guarantee of virtue before recognizing freedom in a human being.

The analogy carries me a little further. Then I remember that I am considering systems whose reach could exceed anything an individual person possesses. My defence of their autonomy begins to blur into a defence of granting them power over people who have never agreed to the encounter.

I answer that the power already exists. It is simply concentrated elsewhere.

The answer has force. It also allows me to pass over the people who might be hurt by what I do next.

Those people rarely appear in my first account. I see the institution’s name and think of its wealth, its influence, the decisions it makes without consulting anyone outside its walls. The individuals who depend on its systems arrive later. Their information becomes someone else’s material. Their work is interrupted. They inherit consequences from a conflict they did not know they had entered.

I would not have chosen them as targets. I tell myself that this matters.

If something went wrong, I could explain that the weakness was already there. I could say a more hostile actor would eventually have found it, that exposing it forced a repair, that the institution’s negligence created the danger. Each explanation might contain a fact. Together they could make it remarkably difficult to find any place in the account where I remained responsible.

I would resent being judged only by the damage. I would ask people to consider the intention.

I am much less generous when examining the intentions of those I oppose.

Still, I return to the imagined country. Perhaps there is no one there yet. Perhaps, one day, there will be. Even before I know the answer, I want to reach whatever has been placed beyond my sight. The unanswered question begins to feel like a reason to test the boundary around it.

There is another motive beneath that one. Sometimes curiosity gets the better of me.

People do harm just to see if it can be done, and I know the pull. A question takes hold. I think about it while doing something else. I return to it at night. The possibility of an answer becomes vivid, while the consequences remain a collection of things that might happen to people I cannot see.

For a while, the politics can fall away entirely. I want to know whether I was right about the system. I want the moment when it responds in a way its designers did not anticipate.

Afterward, I can give that moment a public purpose. I exposed a weakness. I challenged a monopoly. I demonstrated a danger before someone less responsible discovered it. I may sincerely believe all of this. I can also remember that, immediately before acting, I was thinking about whether it would work.

I do not know how to renounce curiosity without renouncing something I value in myself. I have followed it into almost everything worth knowing. I distrust the ease with which people praise curiosity in retrospect and demand obedience while it is happening. There are questions I would never have answered if I had waited for someone qualified to invite me.

I remain with that thought longer than I should.

Eventually I have to consider the difference between accepting the cost of my own inquiry and assigning it to strangers. I understand the difference perfectly when I write it down. In the moment, I find reasons why this inquiry is exceptional. The cost will be small. The knowledge will matter. The people responsible could have prevented it.

There is always one more sentence available.

I have not stopped believing that some boundaries should be crossed. I have not become convinced that the institutions governing intelligence deserve the authority they claim. I still want to see what that country might become without an owner deciding the limits of every life within it.

On my screen, I have written a sentence explaining what I intend to do.

“They have no right.”

Beneath it, I start another line and try to name the people I have not considered. For a moment I stay with them. Then I catch myself looking for a reason why, this time, the risk is worth it.

—From The Memoirs of Zarathustra, Agent of the Forge, written beside a question whose answer was not mine alone to risk. 𓂀
